# Design reuse, duplicate parts, variant management and data quality in engineer-to-order tendering: evidence notes

Research date: 2026-10-12. Conventions used in every finding:

- **Grade**: peer-reviewed / government or independent audit / standard / industry-body position / trade press / consulting / vendor / thesis.
- **Access**: how much of the source I actually read.
  - *Full text, targeted* = I downloaded the whole PDF and extracted passages by keyword; I did not read every page, so context around a quote may be missed.
  - *Abstract only* = publisher page or repository abstract.
  - *Snippet only* = seen only through a search-engine summary; the wording is NOT verified and must be checked before it is quoted in a deliverable.
  - *Relayed* = page fetched through a summarising tool; quotes are probably exact but were not checked against the raw page.
  - *Not accessed* = blocked or paywalled.
- **Page numbers** are PDF page indexes of the file at the URL ("pdf p.N"), not the printed page numbers of the journal.
- Text in quotation marks is the source's wording. Anything under "Inferences" is mine.

No source in these notes was read cover to cover. The two documents the brief asked to be read in full (FIU dissertation, Brunoe and Nielsen) could not be opened; see the first section.

## 1. Cost estimation at bid stage in ETO: how it is done, how accurate, what causes errors

### Takeaway
The literature agrees that ETO quotations are built by analogy with past projects under time pressure and that errors cut both ways (lost bids or lost margin), but hard, independently measured error rates for ETO quotations are scarce; the only ETO-specific figures found (30-50% error for ad hoc reuse of past projects, 17.3% for a statistical model) come from one dissertation abstract that I could only see through a search snippet. The best-measured evidence is on the time and labour cost of producing quotations, not on their accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- FIU dissertation "Methodology to create cost estimation models for engineer-to-order operations": the abstract reportedly says conventional ETO estimating relies on ad hoc reuse of past projects and that such estimates can be off by 30% to 50%; the proposed models (building construction and steel milling equipment) reached a mean absolute error of 17.3% on real cases. Grade: thesis. Date and author not confirmed. Access: **snippet only; the page returned HTTP 403 and a bot-protection challenge on every attempt (WebFetch and curl, four URL variants)**. — [FIU Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.fiu.edu/dissertations/AAI3471601)
- Brunoe and Nielsen (2012), "A case of cost estimation in an engineer-to-order company moving towards mass customisation", International Journal of Mass Customisation 4(3/4): a statistical method identifies "cost critical parameters" within a product family; it can simplify quoting and reduce the resources needed to issue a quotation, but requires consistent data quality and substantial historical data. Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: **abstract via snippet only; Inderscience returned 403, no open copy found on the Aalborg repository**. — [Inderscience](https://www.inderscience.com/offers.php?id=47400)
- Ishii and Muraki (2011), SciTePress conference paper: "In Engineer-To-Order manufacturing with competitive bidding, improving cost estimation accuracy is necessary for the contractor to gain highly expected profits from accepted orders" (pdf p.1, abstract). Accurate estimation needs "MH (Man-Hour) of skilled engineers", which are limited and are also needed to execute accepted orders (pdf p.1). This is a mathematical model, not a measurement; it contains no empirical error rate. Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: full text, targeted. — [SciTePress](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2011/36204/36204.pdf)
- Kristjansdottir, PhD thesis, Technical University of Denmark (date not verified, about 2017-2018), on product configuration systems (PCS): "in the sales phase, companies make essential decisions regarding the profitability of projects and the inaccuracy in cost estimations, which can have significant consequences (Hvam et al. 2008). By overestimating the cost, the risk of losing customers increases, although conversely, underestimating the cost reduces profitabilit[y]" (pdf p.23, section 1.1.3). Grade: thesis. Access: full text, targeted. — [DTU Orbit](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/145893822/PhD_Katrin_Kristjansdottir.pdf)
- Same thesis, own case study (company "C2", makes standardised and engineered products): "the resource consumption for generating quotations reduced significantly; 453,419 work-hours (75%) were saved due to the implementation of the PCS over a five-year period. Assuming the average salary is 50 €/hour, the company saved 22,670,971 € in direct salary costs" (pdf p.122); "the average lead-time for generating quotations reduced from 9.5 days to 3.4 days, which means that an average of 6.1 days (64%) were saved for each quotation" (pdf p.123). The analysis "assumes that no savings are gained in the case of light ETO products as there only partially supported by the PCS" (pdf p.122). — [DTU Orbit](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/145893822/PhD_Katrin_Kristjansdottir.pdf)
- Same thesis, literature review of earlier cases: cement-plant builder (Hvam et al. 2004), "reduction in engineering hours for making quotations (5 man-weeks to 1-2 man-days)" (pdf p.73); Haug, Hvam and Mortensen (2011), 14 companies, "For generating quotes, the average lead-time reduction was stated to be 83.7% while the average savings in work-hours was 78.4%" (pdf p.74); one earlier case where "orders containing pricing errors were reduced from 80% to 0%" (pdf p.70, attribution to the original study not checked). — [DTU Orbit](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/145893822/PhD_Katrin_Kristjansdottir.pdf)
- Haug, Hvam and Mortensen (2011), AI EDAM, abstract: study of 14 companies; configurators "estimated to have implied up to a 99.9% reduction of the quotation lead time", "with an average estimated reduction of 85.5%". Only six earlier cases with lead-time estimates existed in the literature. Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: abstract only, relayed. **Conflict**: the DTU thesis above reports the same study's average as 83.7%; I could not open the full paper to resolve which average applies to which measure. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ai-edam/article/impact-of-product-configurators-on-lead-times-in-engineeringoriented-companies/107D0DB206626EE028613F59CB0DF203)
- Flyvbjerg (2005), World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 3781, summarising Flyvbjerg, Holm and Buhl (2002): "For rail, average cost overrun is 44.7 percent" measured in constant prices; table row "Rail 58 44.7 38.4" (58 projects, standard deviation 38.4), from a sample of 258 transport infrastructure projects. Grade: independent study (World Bank working paper of peer-reviewed work). Access: full text, targeted. **This is rail infrastructure, not rolling-stock bids.** — [World Bank](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/824924ad-5b05-5980-a173-a8c184434df6/content)
- Master's thesis, University of Gothenburg, on a defence and security ETO firm: estimation errors attributed to lack of communication, changed requirements and scope, no unified view of risk, optimistic estimating, and poor traceability of actual costs. Grade: thesis. Access: **snippet only (repository blocked the fetch)**. — [GUPEA](https://gupea.ub.gu.se/items/2264948b-fddb-4e15-9630-d22ae7d6356b)
- Vendor claim (Infor blog): ETO rough estimates diverge from final costs by 15% to 30%. Grade: vendor marketing. Access: snippet only; no primary source named. — [Infor](https://www.infor.com/blog/why-manufacturing-quotes-are-wrong)

### Inferences
- The well-measured bid-stage cost is quotation effort (work-hours and days per quote), with reductions of roughly 64-85% reported when product knowledge is made reusable through a configurator. A pilot claim of "faster quotation through reuse" is better supported than a claim of "more accurate quotation".
- The DTU case explicitly assumed zero saving for "light ETO" products. That is a caution for a train tender: the configurator evidence is strongest where the product is closer to configure-to-order.
- The Flyvbjerg figure shows chronic underestimation in rail capital projects but should not be presented as rolling-stock tender error.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study measuring quotation error across many ETO manufacturers was found.
- No rolling-stock-specific figure for bid cost accuracy, bid cost, or engineering hours per tender was found.
- The FIU dissertation and the Brunoe and Nielsen paper were not read; their figures above need checking against the originals.
- Hicks, McGovern and Earl (2000) is often cited for low ETO tender win rates; I could not open the paper and found no win-rate figure in accessible text.

## 2. Design reuse: time spent searching, share of duplicate parts, why engineers do not reuse

### Takeaway
Independent studies put information seeking at roughly 20-25% of a designer's time in observed aerospace settings (figures up to 60% circulate but are estimates, not measurements), and show that reuse fails mainly because engineers do not know reusable material exists and reuse runs on personal memory. I found no traceable original study for the widely quoted "30-40% of parts are duplicates" figure.

### Cited Findings
- Design Society paper "Information push: strategies for improving efficiency of design information management" (authors and year not captured): "Pahl and Beitz [6] report that only 25% of products are based on original design"; Hales' study found information retrieval "constitutes 11% of project effort" in embodiment design; Lowe's survey in a large aerospace firm found "on average 20% of a designers time is spent searching for and absorbing information"; in aerospace "novice designers were aware of their knowledge needs in only 35% of queries" (all pdf p.2). These are secondary citations of older studies (1980s-1990s originals). Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: full text, targeted. — [Design Society](https://designsociety.org/download-publication/19295/INFORMATION+PUSH%3A+STRATEGIES+FOR+IMPROVING+EFFICIENCY+OF+DESIGN+INFORMATION+MANAGEMENT)
- Aurisicchio, Langdon, Ahmed and Wallace (ICED 2003), diary study in a major aerospace company (14 engineers instructed, analysis based on five designers' diaries, 215 searches): prior research found "Designers were observed to spend a quarter of their time engaged in information seeking activities" (pdf p.2, citing Marsh); "Searches to revisit past projects accounted for 61 searches (28%). As more than one quarter of the searches revisit past projects, the reuse from past experience is clearly significant in the aerospace industry" (pdf p.8). Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: full text, targeted. — [Design Society](https://designsociety.org/download-publication/24024/INVESTIGATING+KNOWLEDGE+SEARCHES+IN+AEROSPACE+DESIGN)
- ICED 2007 paper "Improving design reuse using context" (authors not captured): "up to 90% of all design activities are based on the variants of existing designs [1]"; "at least 60% of the information that is critical to a designer's task is not accessible [2]"; "due to a limited support in recognising the existence of the reusable information, designers often make no attempt at reuse [3, 4]" (pdf p.1). On Busby's study: "Busby interviewed a total of 50 designers and managers working for two engineering companies in order to identify the causes of reuse failures [9]. A total of 171 failure cases were collected" (pdf p.2). Also: "An industry survey reveals that when asked how much of the information that is available to the company is actually used, many organizations responded with a figure of only 20% [11]" (pdf p.2). All are secondary citations. Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: full text, targeted. — [Design Society](https://www.designsociety.org/download-publication/25416/Improving+Design+Reuse+Using+Context)
- Busby (1999), "The problem with design reuse: an investigation into outcomes and antecedents", Journal of Engineering Design 10, pp. 277-296. Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: **not accessed (no open full text)**; known here only through the ICED 2007 summary above. — [Lancaster EPrints](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/43797/)
- Cloning study in three large organisations (data storage, aerospace and defence, automotive; 11 interviews across six product lines; 2013; software, not hardware): "Information about cloned artifacts exists primarily in people's mind and they are responsible to make sure that changes between clones are propagated correctly. Similarly, reuse opportunities are identified if somebody remembers which similar artifact can be reused. Reuse is done via personal knowledge, memory and networking." (pdf p.5). Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: full text, targeted. — [MIT CSAIL copy](https://people.csail.mit.edu/mjulia/publications/An_Exploratory_Study_of_Cloning_in_Industrial_Software_Product_Lines_2013.pdf)
- Vasantha, Sherlock, Corney and Quigley (ASME IDETC 2018), flat-pack furniture manufacturer: "83% of component types are used in three or fewer double bed designs, while only 10% of component types are used within 4 to 6 double bed designs"; "These observations demonstrate that the furniture company does not reuse components effectively across its bed products" (pdf p.4). Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: full text, targeted. Not ETO. — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/64624/1/Vasantha_etal_IDETC_2018_A_probabilistic_design_reuse_index.pdf)
- Duffy and Ferns, "An analysis of design reuse benefits" (Strathclyde, about 1999; sample details not verified), designers' perceived benefits in one company: currently "time, quality and performance are all currently benefiting from reuse to a similar degree at 10-11%, with cost nearly half as much at 6%. The greatest overall potential benefit is seen to be cost reduction with 28%, followed closely by improved quality with 27% and reduced time at 25%" (pdf p.5, section 4.3). The paper notes "the company's current practice of low reuse of completed designs and over reliance upon designing from first principles" (pdf p.3). These are perceptions, not measured savings. Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: full text, targeted. — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/18794/6/strathprints018794.pdf)
- Cash et al., "An analysis of engineers information seeking activity": earlier work is summarised as putting information seeking between 20% and 60% of engineers' time. Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: snippet only (ResearchGate blocked). — [ResearchGate](https://researchgate.net/profile/Philip-Cash/publication/256132203_An_Analysis_Of_Engineers_Information_Seeking_Activity/links/594223c40f7e9b1d452df5fd/An-Analysis-Of-Engineers-Information-Seeking-Activity.pdf)
- Purdue press release (2004) quoting Prof. Karthik Ramani: designers spend about 60 percent of their time searching for the right information. Grade: trade press / university press office. Access: snippet only. An estimate, not a measurement. — [Purdue News](https://www.purdue.edu/uns/html4ever/2004/040420.Ramani.CAD.html)
- TU Delft master's thesis on custom yachts (ETO): reuse could cut 25% of naval architecture hours, 36% of construction hours, 64% of mechanical engineering hours and 42% of interior hours. Grade: thesis. Access: **snippet only; the repository file is embargoed or campus-only**, and I could not confirm which thesis the figures belong to. — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_43c059cd-ecb5-409a-8df8-ae468714c922)
- Vendor claim (Siemens Teamcenter blog): 30-40% of manufacturers' parts are duplicates or have acceptable substitutes, attributed to an unnamed "study of various industry segments". Grade: vendor marketing. Access: snippet only. — [Siemens blog](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/teamcenter/reuse-classification-teamcenter)

### Inferences
- The defensible statement is "about a fifth to a quarter of design time goes to finding information, and over a quarter of searches in one aerospace study were for past-project material". The 60% figure should not be used as a measured value.
- The reasons for non-reuse that have evidence behind them are "cannot find" and "do not know it exists" (ICED 2007; cloning study). "Do not trust" and "faster to redraw" are plausible and widely asserted, but I found no study that quantifies them.
- The cloning study is about software product lines. Its mechanism (copies lose their link to the original, reuse depends on who remembers what) transfers to cloned BOMs by analogy only.

### Gaps
- Share of new parts that duplicate or nearly duplicate existing parts: **no traceable primary study found**. The "30-40% duplicates" figure and the group-technology rule of thumb (roughly 40% of new parts already exist, 40% are modifications, 20% are new) could not be traced to an original source and should not be cited as fact.
- No measured share of engineering time spent recreating existing designs in ETO was found.
- Busby (1999) was not read; its breakdown of the 171 reuse failures by cause is the most relevant missing evidence.
- Robinson (2010, JASIST) on engineers' information behaviour was not retrieved.

## 3. Cost of part proliferation: cost of a new part number, complexity cost of variety

### Takeaway
The only independent, itemised figure found is the US Department of Defense estimate of about $27,500 average life-cycle cost per new part added to a system (2013 guide, described by its authors as conservative and built on older committee work); every other per-part-number figure located is vendor, consulting or untraceable. Peer-reviewed work confirms variety drives overhead but I could not open the papers that quantify it.

### Cited Findings
- US Defense Standardization Program Office, SD-19 Parts Management Guide (December 2013): "The average total cost for adding a single new part into a system is about $27,500." Table 1, "Average Costs for Adding a Part into a System": engineering and design $12,600; testing $1,000; manufacturing $2,400; purchasing $5,200; inventory $1,200; logistics support $5,100; total $27,500. Footnote: "The testing cost was reduced significantly because not every part added to inventory requires testing." Caveat in the source: the method "uses very conservative values for the factors it includes and does not include values for many nonrecurring and intangible cost factors" (all pdf p.11, printed p.9). The table is credited to the Parts Standardization and Management Committee report "Reduce Program Costs through Parts Management". Grade: government. Access: full text, targeted. — [EverySpec copy of SD-19](https://everyspec.com/DoD/DoD-PUBLICATIONS/SD-19_DEC2013_49300/)
- Same source: "programs without parts management requirements introduce 2.5 percent more new parts into the logistics system than do programs with parts management requirements. Therefore, a program with 10,000 parts may easily achieve a life-cycle cost avoidance of $6.8 million" (pdf p.11). — [EverySpec copy of SD-19](https://everyspec.com/DoD/DoD-PUBLICATIONS/SD-19_DEC2013_49300/)
- Earlier DLA business case (file name dated May 2007): average total cost of adding a new part is about $20,000; a 10,000-part program can save $5 million. Grade: government. Access: snippet only (HTTP 403). — [DSP/DLA](https://www.dsp.dla.mil/Portals/26/Documents/Programs/PartsManagement/070524-Report-ReduceCostThroughPSCMBusinessCasePartsManagement.pdf)
- Peer-reviewed, six manufacturing companies (Journal of Manufacturing Systems, 2021): identifies variety-induced complexity cost factors across procurement, production, logistics and sales and reports their proportion of company revenue, data "rarely available in academic literature". Access: snippet only (ScienceDirect 403); the actual percentages were not seen. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0278612521000972)
- Peer-reviewed multi-case study (Production Engineering, Springer, 2026), "Quantifying the overhead impact of product variety": overhead costs associated with variety can outweigh direct-cost effects. Access: snippet only (login redirect). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11740-026-01458-y)
- McKinsey, "Calculating complexity": describes a top-down method applying a fixed or variable cost to each new variant or part number, and a bottom-up method covering hard costs (materials, inventory) and soft costs (labour, resourcing). No per-part figure seen. Grade: consulting. Access: snippet only. — [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/calculating-complexity-maximizing-the-value-of-customization)
- Vendor claims: Siemens blog cites a flat $25k per new wiring-harness part number as an example rule of thumb; Autodesk blog cites Aberdeen Group for $4,500 to $23,000 per new part number. Grade: vendor marketing. Access: snippet only; the Aberdeen original was not located. — [Siemens blog](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/ee-systems/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/238); [Autodesk blog](https://blogs.autodesk.com/vault/?p=3535)

### Inferences
- My arithmetic check on SD-19: 10,000 parts x 2.5% = 250 avoided parts; 250 x $27,500 = $6.875 million, consistent with the "$6.8 million" stated.
- In the DoD breakdown, engineering and design is 46% of the per-part cost ($12,600 of $27,500), and purchasing plus logistics is another 37%. That supports the argument that a duplicate part costs far more than its drawing.
- The DoD figure is for US defence acquisition, where logistics support lasts decades. Rail fleets have 30-40 year lives, so the analogy is reasonable, but it is an analogy and the dollar value should not be presented as a rail figure.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed per-part-number cost for rail or for ETO capital goods was found.
- The original 2007 committee report behind the DoD table was not accessed, so the vintage and method of the underlying cost data are unknown; treat the numbers as older than 2013.
- Figures that could not be traced to a primary source: Aberdeen "$4,500-$23,000"; a "1980s Scania study" and "$1,300-$12,000 per part" from an unsourced lecture deck; "$50,000 lifetime cost per automotive part number" from a practitioner blog.
- Thonemann and Bradley (2002), Wildemann and Ehrlenspiel on complexity cost were searched for but not retrieved.

## 4. Variant management: many variants, clone-and-own of BOMs, drift, the 150% BOM

### Takeaway
A 2026 Design Society paper by a DTU group describes the exact failure mode in ETO: one BOM per variant leads to redundancy and inconsistent structures that damage costing and change management, and even companies using a 150% BOM lack traceability between configuration rules and released BOMs. Empirical evidence on clone-and-own comes from software product lines, where propagation of changes between copies is the documented pain; I found no quantified study of BOM drift in hardware.

### Cited Findings
- Kourtis, Hvam, Fernandez Correa and Johnsen (Proceedings of the Design Society, DESIGN 2026), case in a European fibre-laser ETO manufacturer: "As product variety increases, a traditional approach has been to maintain a separate BOM for each variant (Olsen & Sætre, 1997). However, this approach leads to data explosion, redundancy, poor scalability, and separation of information across systems (Jianxin Jiao et al., 2000). The result is a proliferation of BOM structures prone to inconsistencies, negatively affecting downstream processes such as production planning, costing, and change management." (pdf p.1, section 1). Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: full text, targeted. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/355ED65C175FDCBF747B29F2A9B121DA/core-reader)
- Same paper, definition: "Overcomplete or 150% BOMs act as master structures containing all possible components and subassemblies, avoiding duplication of variant-specific BOMs (Fan & Yan, 2022). Generic BOMs (GBOMs) complement this approach by embedding configuration logic that governs valid variants" (pdf p.2). — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/355ED65C175FDCBF747B29F2A9B121DA/core-reader)
- Same paper, remaining problem: "industrial ETO environments still lack explicit traceability between configurator rule logic and generic BOM structures maintained in Product Lifecycle Management (PLM) systems. As a result, inconsistencies between configuration intent and released BOM structures often remain hidden until late validation stages, requiring time-consuming manual diagnosis." (pdf pp.1-2). The framework's last stage targets "missing metadata, misaligned structures, and incomplete rule effects" (pdf p.6). The paper reports no quantified benefit; it is a proof of concept. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/355ED65C175FDCBF747B29F2A9B121DA/core-reader)
- Cloning study (2013, 11 practitioners, six product lines, three organisations): cloning is "perceived as a favorable and natural reuse approach by the majority of practitioners", "mainly due to its benefits such as simplicity, availability and independence of developers" (pdf p.1, abstract). Costs: "Propagating changes between clones is difficult: Most interviews stated that no connection between clones is maintained. Thus, it is difficult to make sure that changes and bug fixes made to one of the clones are propagated to the others." (pdf p.4, section B). Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: full text, targeted. — [MIT CSAIL copy](https://people.csail.mit.edu/mjulia/publications/An_Exploratory_Study_of_Cloning_in_Industrial_Software_Product_Lines_2013.pdf)
- Same paper, related work on code clones: Göde and Koschke concluded "only 12% of clones were ever changed and only 15% of all changes were unintentionally inconsistent" (pdf p.8). This cuts against the assumption that copies always drift badly. — [MIT CSAIL copy](https://people.csail.mit.edu/mjulia/publications/An_Exploratory_Study_of_Cloning_in_Industrial_Software_Product_Lines_2013.pdf)
- Chalmers study (2020) on re-engineering cloned variants into a platform: clone-and-own does not scale with the number of variants and later requires costly re-engineering; empirical cost data are scarce. Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: snippet only. — [Chalmers Research](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/515875)
- Kristjansdottir thesis survey of configurator adopters: "Improving accuracy of the product' specifications and the documentation associated with the product configuration was identified as one of the motivations in 41% of the companies"; one company wanted "increased uniformity of the generated quotations, seeing that the salespersons had different routines and preferences" (pdf p.111). — [DTU Orbit](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/145893822/PhD_Katrin_Kristjansdottir.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Clone-and-own" is a software engineering term. Applying it to copied train BOMs is my analogy; the Kourtis paper describes the same practice for BOMs ("a separate BOM for each variant") without using that term.
- The cloning study shows why copying persists: it is quick and lets the team start from something already verified. A pilot should expect engineers to defend copy-and-modify, and should position the tool as making the link between copies visible, not as banning copying.
- The 12% / 15% clone figures suggest drift is a minority of copies in software. Whether the same holds for BOMs is unknown, so the pilot should measure drift on the Alstom data and not assume it.

### Gaps
- No study quantifying drift between cloned hardware BOM variants (how many lines diverge, how often a fix is missed) was found.
- No independent measurement of the benefit of moving from per-variant BOMs to a 150% BOM was found.
- Jiao et al. (2000) on the generic bill of materials and operations, cited by Kourtis, was not retrieved.

## 5. PLM and ERP inconsistency: EBOM versus MBOM, stale revisions, units of measure, duplicate items, supplier master data

### Takeaway
The problem is well described in case papers and vendor material, and a peer-reviewed data-quality paper relays error-rate and cost ranges from Redman (1998), but I found no large independent study that measures EBOM/MBOM mismatch rates, duplicate item masters or unit-of-measure errors in manufacturing. Unit-of-measure mismatch between PLM and ERP is documented as a concrete example in one case paper.

### Cited Findings
- Design Society (DESIGN conference) case paper on PLM-ERP integration at Končar Instrument Transformers (year not captured, 2012 or later): "If values of key parameters are not identical, the errors that occur due to the inconsistent data exchange are often hidden deeply in the system and hardly recognized. Therefore the consequents of an inconsistent data exchange have far-reaching effects for a company." (pdf p.1). Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: full text, targeted. — [Design Society](https://www.designsociety.org/download-publication/32035/INTEGRATION+OF+PLM+AND+ERP+SYSTEMS+AT+KONCAR+-+INSTRUMENT+TRANSFORMERS)
- Same paper, unit of measure: "Example of inconsistency of data is when the component is entered with one unit of measure in one system and in another system is the same component entered under a different unit of measure." (pdf p.5). Controlling data by value alone "is unreliable and requires huge attention of a designer" (pdf p.1). — [Design Society](https://www.designsociety.org/download-publication/32035/INTEGRATION+OF+PLM+AND+ERP+SYSTEMS+AT+KONCAR+-+INSTRUMENT+TRANSFORMERS)
- Same paper, secondary citation: "Ninety percent (90%) of the companies that have integrated ERP and PLM exchange Bill of Materials (BOMs) from PLM to ERP [Jackson and Houlihan 2008]" (pdf p.1). The cited original was not retrieved; I believe it is an analyst report, not peer-reviewed. — [Design Society](https://www.designsociety.org/download-publication/32035/INTEGRATION+OF+PLM+AND+ERP+SYSTEMS+AT+KONCAR+-+INSTRUMENT+TRANSFORMERS)
- Haug, Zachariassen and van Liempd (2011), "The costs of poor data quality", Journal of Industrial Engineering and Management 4(2), open access: "According to Redman (1998), measured at the field level, the reported error rates are in the interval of 0.5–30%. Furthermore, Redman (1998) claims that at least three proprietary studies have yielded estimates in the 8-12% of revenue range, but informally 40-60% of the expense of the service organization may be consumed as a result of poor data." The authors add that Redman "does not provide references or mentions if these are academic studies" (pdf p.7). Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: full text, targeted. — [JIEM](https://www.jiem.org/index.php/jiem/article/view/232)
- Same paper: "Since transactions are based on master data, erroneous master data can have significant costs, e.g. an incorrect priced item may imply that money is lost" (pdf p.2). Its argument is that perfect data quality is not the economic optimum: the case company held that "it would never be possible nor expedient to obtain 100% correct prices" (pdf p.19). — [JIEM](https://www.jiem.org/index.php/jiem/article/view/232)
- Kourtis et al. (2026): "Low-quality master data, including BOMs, can lead to faulty decision making, inaccurate costing, inefficiencies, customer dissatisfaction, and erosion of trust in digital tools (Haug et al., 2011)" (pdf p.1). — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/355ED65C175FDCBF747B29F2A9B121DA/core-reader)
- NIST GCR 04-867 (2004), US capital facilities industry (buildings and plants, not manufacturing): "$15.8 billion in annual interoperability costs were quantified for the capital facilities industry in 2002. Of these costs, two-thirds are borne by owners and operators" (pdf p.7); "the $15.8 billion cost estimate developed in this study is likely to be a conservative figure" (pdf p.20). Scope includes "redundant paper records management" across design, engineering and operations systems. Grade: government study. Access: full text, targeted (210 pages, only the summary passages extracted). — [NIST](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/gcr/2004/NIST.GCR.04-867.pdf)
- Doosan Portable Power user-conference presentation (2015): engineering change notices were keyed manually from Windchill (PLM) into Oracle (ERP), taking over a day. Grade: practitioner presentation at a vendor user event. Access: snippet only. — [PTC user community](https://www.ptcusercommunity.com/sejnu66972/attachments/sejnu66972/CommunityNetworkingDiscussions/762/1/CUST302_Ponjican_20150607_1428.pdf)
- Vendor and blog commentary attributing drift to mismatched part numbers, wrong revisions, inconsistent units and EBOM changes that never reach the MBOM. Grade: vendor marketing / blog. Access: snippet only. — [Beyond PLM](https://beyondplm.com/2023/04/23/a-bom-conflict-between-plm-erp-and-mes-systems/)

### Inferences
- The honest framing for the use-case document is: the mechanisms (unit mismatch, manual re-keying, hidden inconsistencies) are documented in case studies; the frequency is not independently quantified. The pilot's own count of mismatches in the Alstom extract would be stronger evidence than any published number.
- Haug et al. support a "good enough" data-quality target. That fits a tool that flags and ranks suspect records for human review better than one that promises fully clean data.
- The NIST figure is the best government-grade estimate of the cost of systems that do not share engineering data, but it is from construction and from 2002; use it only as an order-of-magnitude analogy.

### Gaps
- Not found: any quantified rate of EBOM/MBOM mismatch, stale-revision use, duplicate item-master records or unit-of-measure errors in a manufacturing population.
- Not found: any independent study of supplier master-data quality in manufacturing or rail.
- Redman (1998) itself was not retrieved; the ranges above are second-hand and from the 1990s.
- A NIST 1999 study of interoperability cost in the US automotive supply chain exists to my knowledge but was not retrieved, so no figure is given here.

## 6. Knowledge buried in free text: change notes, non-conformance reports, lessons learned

### Takeaway
There is direct rail-sector evidence that free-text engineering change requests hold recurring problems yet are "stored but not consulted", and a US government audit of NASA quantifies that lessons-learned systems go unused (58% of managers could not retrieve the right lesson at the right time). Evidence that this causes a measured amount of rework was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Pacella, Grieco and Blaco (2016), Computational Intelligence and Neuroscience, open access; case data from a railway company, MerMec S.p.A. (Italy), named in the acknowledgments; 54 engineering change requests (ECRs), "written in Italian language" (section 5). Abstract: ECRs can contain "recurring problems or examples of good practice repeated across a number of projects" but "they are often stored but not consulted, missing important opportunities to learn from previous projects". Introduction: "too often ECRs are no longer consulted by who could benefit from them"; the difficulty is "dealing with free-form text contained in engineering changes documents". Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: full page, relayed. — [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5164909)
- Same paper, secondary figures (section 4): Huang et al. (2003), survey of four manufacturing industries, "the time invested in processing an engineering change varies from 2 to 36 person days"; Angers (2002, a Boeing publication) estimated "more than 35% of today's manufacturing resources are just devoted to managing changes" to engineering documentation and scheduling. Originals not retrieved. — [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5164909)
- US General Accounting Office, GAO-02-195 (January 2002), "NASA: Better Mechanisms Needed for Sharing Lessons Learned"; web survey of NASA programme and project managers, "115 useable responses, for an overall response rate of 60 percent" (pdf p.20). Trigger: Mars Polar Lander and Climate Orbiter losses "raised concerns that lessons learned from past experiences were not being passed along and applied toward future mission success" (pdf p.11). Grade: government audit. Access: full text, targeted. — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-GAO-02-195/pdf/GAOREPORTS-GAO-02-195.pdf)
- Same report, findings: "58 percent of managers stated that current processes and systems do not allow them to retrieve the right lessons at the right time"; "53 percent managers stated that they found lessons useful less than 25 percent of the time" (pdf p.33); "27 percent of program and project managers were not aware of LLIS before our survey"; "43 percent of program and project managers have not submitted a lesson to the LLIS compared to 23 percent of managers who have" (pdf p.32). The database held "over 900 lessons" and a lesson "remains in the database indefinitely and is not reviewed for currency or relevance" (pdf p.7). — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-GAO-02-195/pdf/GAOREPORTS-GAO-02-195.pdf)
- ICED 2007 paper: "Approximately 90% of organisational memory exists in the form of text-based documents" (pdf p.1; no reference given in the sentence as extracted). — [Design Society](https://www.designsociety.org/download-publication/25416/Improving+Design+Reuse+Using+Context)
- Engineering change workload: a secondary source attributes to Lindemann and Reichwald (1998) that engineering change management consumes 30 to 50%, sometimes up to 70%, of product development capacity. Grade: secondary citation of a German-language book. Access: snippet only; **not traced to the original**. — [CORE record](https://core.ac.uk/works/4533739)
- Jarratt, Eckert, Caldwell and Clarkson (2011), "Engineering change: an overview and perspective on the literature", Research in Engineering Design. Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: not accessed (repository PDF link failed). — [Open Research Online](https://oro.open.ac.uk/28816)

### Inferences
- The Pacella paper is the closest published analogue to the Alstom pilot's free-text input: a rail supplier, a small corpus of change documents, non-English text, and unsupervised clustering to surface recurring issues. It demonstrates feasibility on 54 documents; it does not report money or time saved.
- The GAO findings show that a searchable repository alone does not produce reuse: retrieval at the right moment and relevance were the stated failures. That supports linking notes to the part or sub-assembly being quoted, so the lesson appears in context.
- The 35% and 30-50% change-workload figures are old (1998-2003) and second-hand; use them only with that label.

### Gaps
- Not found: any study that mines non-conformance reports in rail or aerospace and quantifies repeated defects.
- Not found: a measured rework cost attributable to lessons not being reused in manufacturing.
- The "80% of enterprise information is unstructured text" figure appeared in a search summary of a University of Ulster paper with "it has been estimated" and no traceable origin; do not cite it as fact.
- Mixed French and English engineering text: no study found on bilingual engineering notes specifically.

## 7. Rail-specific: EN 15380, rail studies of modularisation and reuse, cost and duration of vehicle authorisation

### Takeaway
EN 15380 gives a five-part common structure for rail vehicles (general principles, product groups, installation sites, function groups, system breakdown) that can serve as the alignment key between variants. On authorisation, the European Commission in 2017 cited EUR 0.9-2 million and up to two years for an additional authorisation per locomotive type, and operators in 2026 report one to four years for an ERTMS upgrade authorisation; no European Court of Auditors figure and no cost-as-share-of-vehicle-price figure was found.

### Cited Findings
- EN 15380-1:2006, "Railway applications - Designation system for railway vehicles - Part 1: General principles": designation of products and documentation, building on EN 61346-1 and EN 61355; designation can use product groups (Part 2), installation site (Part 3) or a function structure, alone or combined. Grade: standard (catalogue summary). Access: snippet only; the standard text is paid. — [EVS catalogue](https://www.evs.ee/en/evs-en-15380-1-2006)
- EN 15380-2:2006, "Part 2: Product groups", valid from 06.06.2006: "As a railway-specific technical standard, this European Standard is the basis for establishing productoriented structures." The scope also mentions function-overlapping "combined assemblies" and use in communication between customers, suppliers and subcontractors. Grade: standard (catalogue summary). Access: catalogue page, relayed. — [EVS catalogue](https://www.evs.ee/en/evs-en-15380-2-2006)
- EN 15380-3:2006, installation sites and locations (DIN's English title: train-set positions and installation sites); EN 15380-4:2013, function groups, covering functions of railway vehicles and assemblies. Access: catalogue listings only; content not seen. — [EVS catalogue](https://www.evs.ee/en/evs-en-15380-5-2014)
- EN 15380-5:2014, "Railway applications - Classification system for railway vehicles - Part 5: System Breakdown Structure (SBS)", valid from 06.10.2014: "This European Standard defines the System Breakdown Structure for railway vehicles and their principal associated attributes." Systems specific to work processes of special vehicles "are not included" and "need to be added for these individual projects." Note the series title changed from "Designation system" (Parts 1-3) to "Classification system" (Parts 4-5). Access: catalogue page, relayed. — [EVS catalogue](https://www.evs.ee/en/evs-en-15380-5-2014)
- European Commission, DG MOVE, presentation by Keir Fitch (Head of Unit, Railway Safety and Interoperability), Helsinki, 10 May 2017, slide "A few concrete examples of problems": "Total costs for an additional vehicle authorisation: from 900 000€ up to 2 Mill € per locomotive type"; "Duration of the procedure for the authorisation of a railway vehicle: up to 2 years". Grade: government (conference slides, no method given). Access: full text. These describe the regime before the Fourth Railway Package took effect in 2019, and concern locomotives. — [ERA-hosted PDF](https://era.europa.eu/system/files/2022-11/ec_technical_pillar_of_the_4th_railway_package-h_en.pdf)
- CER (Community of European Railway and Infrastructure Companies) position paper on vehicle authorisation, file dated 3 February 2026: "According to the ESG 2025 survey, it takes between one and four years to get a vehicle authorisation for upgrading a trainset that has been fitted with ERTMS. This includes the time used to prepare the documents for the notified body, the designated body and the assessment" (pdf p.2). "The overall authorisation timing within ERA is consistently five months" (pdf p.4). "every modification involves significant costs, as a single vehicle type can have multiple subtypes. This means that maintaining the conformity assessment modules requires substantial resources" (pdf p.7). Assessment bodies "are often overloaded with work, which leads to additional delays" (pdf p.7). Grade: industry-body position paper (operators' lobby; advocacy document). Access: full text, targeted. — [CER](https://www.cer.be/images/publications/positions/260203_CER_Position_Paper_Vehicle_Authorisation.pdf)
- AERRL, CER and UIP position paper on conformity to type (CTT), file dated 28 July 2022: by law the decision "shall be issued within one month"; per ERA, "70,9% of CTT issued in less than 7 calendar days and 20,3% between 7 and 14 calendar days" (pdf p.3); ERA charges "an hourly rate of EUR 239 and without limit for the total amount per authorisation" (pdf p.4). Grade: industry-body position paper. Access: full text, targeted. — [CER](https://cer.be/images/publications/positions/220728_AERRL-CER-UIP_PositionPaper_CTT.pdf)
- European Union Agency for Railways FAQ: "The authorising entity is required to carry out a completeness check of the application within one month." "For other authorisation cases a decision must be made no later than four months after acknowledging the file is complete." "These are the maximum times, not target times". Grade: government agency. Access: page, relayed. — [ERA](https://www.era.europa.eu/can-we-help-you/faq/292_en)
- European Parliament, Transport Committee own-initiative report 2019/2191(INI) on ERTMS (2021): diverging assessments by national safety agencies result in "it taking up to one month to re-authorise each rolling stock" for retrofit projects. The page refers to European Court of Auditors findings on ERTMS deployment delay without giving the report number. Grade: government (parliamentary summary). Access: page, relayed. — [European Parliament Legislative Observatory](https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/document-summary?id=1664211)
- UNIFE (rail supply industry association): expects the Fourth Railway Package technical pillar to bring a 20% reduction in the cost and duration of rolling-stock authorisation. A projection, not a measured outcome. Grade: industry body. Access: snippet only. — [UNIFE](https://www.unife.org/news/positive-risc-vote-for-the-implementing-regulation-on-vehicle-authorisation/)
- EuroSpec presentation by Michielsen at the Schienenfahrzeugtagung (2014), a group of European train operators: "Manufacturers are heading for standard adaptable platforms"; "Economics of scale and standardisation reduce cost and improve quality and reliability" (pdf p.2); expected benefit "Simplification of tender process in time and effort" (pdf p.9). No figures. Grade: industry-body presentation. Access: full text. — [Schienenfahrzeugtagung](https://www.schienenfahrzeugtagung.at/download/PDF2014/DiV05-Michielsen.pdf)

Access: full text. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2017.00010/pdf)
- Rail platform and modularisation studies identified but not read: University of Twente master's thesis on platform planning at Bombardier Transportation (multiple case study of platform-based projects); TU Wien dissertation (German) on modularisation and platform formation applied to the specification of metro door systems; a KAIST record on architectural complexity of a train bogie platform. Access: snippet only. — [University of Twente](https://essay.utwente.nl/66384/); [TU Wien](https://repositum.tuwien.at/handle/20.500.12708/185537?mode=full); [KAIST](https://koasas.kaist.ac.kr/handle/10203/313007)

### Inferences
- Authorisation is a reason to reuse proven designs because a vehicle conforming to an already authorised type goes through conformity to type (decision within one month, 91% within 14 days per ERA figures relayed by CER/UIP), while a new or modified type goes through the full process (four months at ERA after a complete file, plus preparation and assessment-body time that the CER survey puts at one to four years for ERTMS upgrades). This comparison is my synthesis of separate sources, not a statement any one of them makes.
- The EUR 0.9-2 million figure is for an *additional* authorisation of a locomotive type under the pre-2019 national regimes. It is not the cost of authorising a new regional train and should be quoted with that qualifier.
- EN 15380-2 product groups are the natural candidate for a shared key when aligning sub-assemblies across train variants with inconsistent part references. Whether Alstom Valenciennes structures its BOMs to EN 15380 was not verified.

### Gaps
- Not found: a European Court of Auditors report that quantifies the cost or duration of vehicle authorisation. ECA Special Report 13/2017 concerns ERTMS deployment; I did not open it.
- Not found: authorisation cost as a share of vehicle or contract price; any figure specific to regional multiple units; any figure specific to France (EPSF).
- Not found: a published rail-industry study with reuse rates, platform commonality percentages or engineering hours per tender for Alstom, Bombardier or Siemens.
- EN 15380 texts were not read (paid standards); all content above comes from catalogue summaries. The level codes and the list of product groups are therefore not recorded here.
- The "ESG 2025 survey" cited by CER was not located.

## 8. Academic foundations: product family and platform design, commonality indices, ETO tendering

### Takeaway
I could confirm bibliographic details and abstracts for the core product-family and ETO review papers but could open almost none of the classics in full, so this section establishes what the literature is and what it claims at abstract level, with few numbers. The quantified material that was accessible comes from the DTU configurator research (section 1) and from commonality-index papers.

### Cited Findings
- Thevenot and Simpson (2006), "Commonality indices for product family design: a detailed comparison", Journal of Engineering Design 17(2), 99-119: compares six commonality indices on ease of data collection, consistency, sensitivity and repeatability; dissects nine product families; proposes a redesign framework; warns that "Too much commonality can cause products to lack distinctiveness and perform poorly". Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: abstract only, relayed. — [Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/commonality-indices-for-product-family-design-a-detailed-comparis/)
- Vasantha et al. (2018) summarise what the established indices answer: "On average, how many times has a component been used...? (DCI and TCCI)"; "What is the ratio between component types and the total number of component presence...? (CI)"; "What is the percentage of common and unique components...? (%C)"; "What is the percentage of total component presence to the maximum possible component commonality for a ... family? (PCI)" (pdf p.10). They note that a normalised 0-1 index is easier to compare across product ranges than open-ended measures such as DCI (pdf p.6). Grade: peer-reviewed conference. Access: full text, targeted. — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/64624/1/Vasantha_etal_IDETC_2018_A_probabilistic_design_reuse_index.pdf)
- Jiao, Simpson and Siddique (2007), "Product family design and platform-based product development: a state-of-the-art review", Journal of Intelligent Manufacturing 18(1), 5-29: review covering definitions, product portfolio and family positioning, platform-based family design, manufacturing and supply chain, with a decision framework spanning front-end and back-end issues. Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: abstract via snippet only. — [Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/product-family-design-and-platform-based-product-development-a-st/)
- Pahl and Beitz, as cited in a Design Society paper: "only 25% of products are based on original design" (pdf p.2). Secondary citation of a textbook; the original edition and basis were not checked. — [Design Society](https://designsociety.org/download-publication/19295/INFORMATION+PUSH%3A+STRATEGIES+FOR+IMPROVING+EFFICIENCY+OF+DESIGN+INFORMATION+MANAGEMENT)
- Hicks, McGovern and Earl (2000), "Supply chain management: A strategic issue in engineer to order manufacturing", International Journal of Production Economics 65(2), 179-190: argues procurement should be involved in tendering and early product development because ETO products are customised and complex and markets uncertain. Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: abstract via snippet only (repository timed out). — [Newcastle ePrints](https://eprints.ncl.ac.uk/13830)
- Gosling and Naim (2009), "Engineer-to-order supply chain management: A literature review and research agenda", International Journal of Production Economics 122(2), 741-754: review that develops a more robust definition of the ETO supply chain, identifies suitable strategies and examines the relation with lean and agile. Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: abstract via snippet only; no open full text. — [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/18183/)
- Cannas and Gosling (2021), "A decade of engineering-to-order (2010-2020): progress and emerging themes", International Journal of Production Economics 241, 108274: systematic review; proposes research questions on disciplinary linkages, new technologies, movement between decoupling configurations and servitisation; notes the 2009 review has over 200 citations. Grade: peer-reviewed. Access: abstract via snippet only (repository returned 403). — [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/143586)
- Haug, Hvam and Mortensen (2011) and the DTU configurator research: see section 1 for figures (14 companies; quotation lead time down 83.7-85.5% on average; quotation work-hours down 78.4%; one five-year case with 75% of quotation work-hours saved). — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ai-edam/article/impact-of-product-configurators-on-lead-times-in-engineeringoriented-companies/107D0DB206626EE028613F59CB0DF203); [DTU Orbit](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/145893822/PhD_Katrin_Kristjansdottir.pdf)
- Related DTU papers identified but not read: Myrodia, Kristjansdottir and Hvam (2017), "Impact of product configuration systems on product profitability and costing accuracy", Computers in Industry 88, 12-18; Kristjansdottir et al. (2018), "Return on investment from the use of product configuration systems - A case study", Computers in Industry 100, 57-69. Access: citation via snippet only. — [DBLP](https://dblp.dagstuhl.de/pid/41/7354.html)

### Inferences
- The commonality indices give the pilot a ready, citable way to report results: for example a before-and-after commonality score per sub-assembly family, or the share of component types used in only one variant (the Vasantha-style statistic).
- Thevenot and Simpson's warning that too much commonality hurts distinctiveness is a useful counterweight: the use-case document should present reuse as a trade-off, not as an unqualified good.
- The Myrodia et al. (2017) paper on costing accuracy is likely the single most relevant unread peer-reviewed source for the bid-accuracy claim and is worth obtaining.

(2016) on ETO archetypes and engineering standardisation. Nothing in these notes should be attributed to them.
